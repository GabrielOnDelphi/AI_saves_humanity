# Proposal · Defending Democracy — Stopping the Slide Before It Locks In

**Status:** Draft for debate · **Last updated:** 2026-06-29 · **Door 2** of [proposal 00](../00-how-change-happens/). The fix for [#14 Democratic backsliding](../../problems/14-democratic-backsliding/); leans on [#05 Governance](../../problems/05-governance-democracy/), [#10 Information](../../problems/10-information-truth/), [#01 Inequality](../../problems/01-economic-inequality/).

> The owner's question: *"how to prevent a country from turning from democracy to dictatorship."* The [#14 problem folder](../../problems/14-democratic-backsliding/) shows the *what* (referee capture, norm erosion, the four warning signs, the measured U.S. slide). This folder is the *what-to-do*.

> **Companion — the long view: [`lessons-from-collapse.md`](lessons-from-collapse.md).** What the fall of Rome and the Tainter/Turchin collapse models say about whether the U.S. can actually decline; the straight answer to the owner's *"how much was the Church to blame?"* (it did **not** cause Rome's fall — the more-Christian Eastern empire lasted ~1,000 years longer — but **state-compelled** religion is the real lesson); and where the 2025–26 Texas religious-school laws fit.

## 1. The core insight — defense is the spine, run in reverse

[Proposal 00](../00-how-change-happens/) found that entrenched power falls when the people it depends on **stop cooperating** (Chenoweth's defection mechanism). Backsliding is the mirror image: **an elected leader can only dismantle a democracy if the referees and enforcers comply.** Judges, election officials, civil servants, prosecutors, soldiers, and editors each face a choice to obey an overreach or refuse it. A backslide *succeeds* when they comply (often slowly, each telling themselves it's not yet the line) and *fails* when enough of them hold — as when courts and election officials across both parties declined to overturn the 2020 U.S. result despite intense pressure. **So the question "how do we defend democracy?" reduces to: how do we make refusal more likely, earlier, by the people whose compliance a power grab requires?**

## 2. The timing paradox (why this is hard)

The tools to resist a power grab — independent courts, a mobilizable public, protected whistleblowers, a funded free press — must exist *before* the grab. But the political will to build them only arrives *after* the threat is undeniable, by which point the grab is already removing them. Defense therefore can't be reactive. It has to be **pre-committed**: things set up now that trigger automatically later, when the actors who'd resist may be intimidated, fired, or gone.

## 3. The three layers

**Layer A — Insulate the referees (structural).** Independent, multi-party election administration; staggered fixed judicial terms so no single leader packs a court; non-partisan prosecution; inspectors-general who can't be fired without cause and notice; civil-service protections against political purges. *The goal:* make the referees expensive to capture and slow to corrupt.

**Layer B — Codify the norms into rules (legal).** Levitsky & Ziblatt's two life-support norms — **mutual toleration** and **forbearance** — die quietly. Where possible, convert them into hard constraints: cap emergency powers and sunset them automatically; bind the executive's spending to appropriations; limit self-pardon and pardon-for-silence; ban mid-decade gerrymanders; require adverse-court-ruling compliance reporting. *The goal:* remove the discretion that forbearance used to govern.

**Layer C — Pre-commit the civil-society response (mobilization).** The empirically strongest brake on a power grab is broad, disciplined, **nonviolent** non-cooperation (proposal 00's ~2:1 success finding). But spontaneous movements are slow and easy to provoke into violence (which triggers the backfire-in-reverse: it *legitimizes* crackdown). So the response must be **organized in advance**: pre-agreed bright lines that trigger action, trained marshals for nonviolent discipline, cross-partisan coalitions (defection by *unexpected* allies is what shifts power), strike funds, and legal-defense networks. *The goal:* a response that is fast, broad, and disciplined on day one — not improvised on day thirty.

## 4. The honest case

These layers attack the mechanism directly: backslides need referee compliance (A raises its cost), exploit discretion (B removes it), and depend on a passive public (C pre-arranges the refusal). None requires inventing new theory — each is drawn from documented cases where democracies *held* or *recovered* (Poland's partial reversal; the survival of contested elections where officials refused to bend).

## 5. Objections (strongest first)

- **"Whoever holds power won't pass reforms that constrain power."** The killer objection — and the reason this is a *Door 2* proposal, not a Door 1 wish. You cannot rely on the threatened incumbent to sign these. They get enacted in the **window after a scare and before capture completes** (a near-miss, a lost election, a coalition moment), or by **sub-national** governments (states, cities) and **non-state** actors (bar associations, unions, the press) who don't need the capturer's permission. Layer C in particular needs *no* legislation.
- **"Codifying forbearance is rigid and gameable."** True — you cannot write a rule for every abuse, and a determined bad actor lawyers around fixed rules. Response: codify only the highest-leverage, most-abused discretions (spending, emergency powers, pardons, redistricting); leave the rest to a *living* civil-society tripwire (Layer C), which adapts where rules can't.
- **"Pre-committed mobilization is itself destabilizing — a standing threat to flood the streets."** It can be. The mitigation is the discipline requirement: the trigger is **specific, public, and high** (e.g. defying a final court order, cancelling/voiding an election), and the method is strictly nonviolent — precisely so it reads as defense of the rules, not a partisan coup attempt. A vague or low trigger would indeed be a problem.
- **"This is just partisan resistance dressed up as principle."** The defense is content-neutrality (see [#14](../../problems/14-democratic-backsliding/)): the same tripwires must bind *your* side. A reform you'd refuse to apply when your party holds power isn't a democratic safeguard — it's a weapon, and should be rejected here.

## 6. Improved / alternative versions

- **Proportional representation** as the upstream fix: it lowers the winner-take-all stakes that make each side tolerate its own leader's abuses. Slower and bigger than Layers A–C, but it drains the polarization that powers backsliding rather than just blocking the symptom.
- **Pro-democracy business and market pressure:** capital flight and credit downgrades have ended power grabs that street protest alone could not. An under-used, fast-acting form of Layer C.
- **International ratchets:** EU-style membership conditionality changed Poland's calculus. Weak where no such club exists, but real where it does.

## 7. What we'd measure

- **Referee independence** before vs. after reforms (capture is slow — watch the trend).
- **Executive compliance with adverse court rulings** (the single cleanest backsliding tripwire).
- **Coalition breadth** of any pre-committed response — is it cross-partisan, or just one team? (Cross-partisan is the one that works.)
- **Whether the tripwires bind symmetrically** — do they constrain the side that wrote them?

## 8. Transition path (who blocks this · which door · smallest first move)

- **Who blocks it:** the incumbent who benefits from weak referees and live discretion; and the *partisans of both sides* who want the safeguards to bind only the other team.
- **Which door:** **Door 2 itself.** Critically, it routes *around* the captured center — sub-national governments, courts, professional bodies, unions, business, and the press can each act without the capturer's sign-off. Layer C needs no law at all.
- **Smallest first move:** **pre-commit one bright line now, cross-partisan, while it's cheap.** A public, signed agreement among otherwise-opposed actors that *one* specific act (defying a final court order; refusing to certify a fair election) triggers a named, nonviolent, coordinated response. It costs nothing today, requires no incumbent's permission, and builds the muscle and the coalition *before* the moment they're needed — directly defeating the timing paradox (§2).

## 9. Open questions

- What trigger is **high enough** to stay credible and non-partisan, yet **low enough** to fire before the tools to respond are gone?
- Can Layer A (insulated referees) be built fast enough to matter, given capture is already underway in the flagged cases?
- Does sub-national defense (states, cities) actually constrain a determined national executive, or just delay it?
- Is there a historical case of a democracy that **pre-committed** a defense and was measurably safer for it — or is this all reactive in practice?
