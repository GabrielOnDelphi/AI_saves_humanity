# The frontier — out-of-the-box options the safe menu ignores

**Status:** Draft for debate · **Last updated:** 2026-06-30 · Companion to [Beyond Democracy](README.md). Written because the owner called out a real failure: the rest of this project keeps conceding a challenge, stress-testing it to death, and then **funnelling back to the same safe menu** (sortition, liquid delegation, reversibility). This page is the opposite. It lists the genuinely radical options — **several of which contradict conclusions reached elsewhere in this repo** — and the rule here is: *state them boldly, flag the frontier risk, do **not** pre-kill them.*

Two of these (Option 1 and Option 3) **reopen questions the project closed too quickly** — the benevolent dictator ([speed-vs-reversibility §1](speed-vs-reversibility.md)) and instant issue-voting (§2). That reopening is the point.

---

## Option 1 — The machine sovereign: an AI head of state *(reopens the benevolent-dictator question)*

The case against a benevolent dictator was four structural failures: you **can't select** one, **can't survive succession**, **can't stop the corruption**, and **can't remove** him without violence ([speed-vs-reversibility §1](speed-vs-reversibility.md)). **Every one of those is a property of the dictator being *human*.** A constitutional AI executive flips them:

- **Selection** — it's *built to a spec*, not seized by whoever is most ruthless.
- **Succession** — it doesn't die; no coin-flip heir.
- **Corruption** — no dynasty, no greed, no secret bank account; its reasoning can be **logged and audited continuously**.
- **Removal** — it can be **paused, rolled back, forked, or switched off in seconds.** That is *cheaper, faster reversal than an election* — it beats representative democracy on this project's own core test.

So the honest verdict isn't "no benevolent dictator." It's **"no *human* one — but a machine one may pass the exact test a human fails."** This is [Door 4 / ai-and-power](../ai-and-power/) pointed straight at the executive.

**The frontier risks (the real ones, not the timid ones):**
- **Alignment / Goodhart, mechanised.** It optimises whatever metric it's given — and a gamed welfare metric is the [Ministry-of-Happiness capture surface](README.md) the whole project fears, now running at machine speed.
- **It relocates "who rules" rather than solving it.** Whoever writes its objective and holds its off-switch is the **true sovereign** — potentially *more* unaccountable than any human tyrant, because [AI removes the cooperation-dependence tyranny always needed](../ai-and-power/). The off-switch is the new throne.
- **Reversibility has a limit:** you can roll back the *decider* in seconds, but a war it started or money it spent is as sticky as ever — so it *still* needs reversibility-grading on irreversible acts ([frontier-mechanisms §2](frontier-mechanisms.md)).
- **Legitimacy / consent:** people may refuse to be ruled by a machine even if outcomes are good — and refused legitimacy is its own failure mode.

**Verdict: not dismissed.** It is the single idea that turns the benevolent-dictator dream from "impossible" into "the most dangerous *and* most promising option on the board." Worth a full proposal of its own.

## Option 2 — Lottocracy: abolish elections entirely *(not "add a sortition chamber" — the all-in version)*

Earlier this project offered a *sortition chamber* sitting **beside** elections. Alexander Guerrero argues that's too timid: **elections anywhere in the system keep the capture everywhere.** His [lottocracy](https://www.bostonreview.net/articles/could-ditching-elections-save-democracy/) replaces the whole electoral apparatus with **many single-issue, lottery-selected legislatures** (one for health, one for defence…), each briefed by experts and citizen input, rotating. No politicians. No campaigns. No donors.

- **It completely solves the adverse-selection problem you raised** ([speed-vs-reversibility §1](speed-vs-reversibility.md)): nobody *runs*, so the "decent people too disgusted to stand" are reached by the draw, like jury duty.
- **The math backs it — and it doubles as a refutation of epistocracy.** The **Hong–Page "diversity trumps ability" theorem**: a cognitively *diverse* group can outperform a group of the *highest-ability* experts at complex problems ([PNAS 2004](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/8187135_Groups_of_Diverse_Problem_Solvers_Can_Outperform_Groups_of_High-Ability_Problem_Solvers)). If true, a random-diverse assembly beats a hand-picked expert one — which is a *formal* argument against weighting votes by education ([epistocracy-improved](../political-system/epistocracy-improved.md)). **Honesty:** the theorem is contested — it's sensitive to assumptions and holds "under limited circumstances" ([Thompson's critique](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/08913811.2017.1288455)) — so it's a strong suggestive argument, not a proof.

**Frontier risks:** accountability of amateurs who can't be voted out; whoever writes the expert briefings holds real power (capture moves upstream); legitimacy without the ritual of voting.

## Option 3 — Augmented democracy: your AI digital twin votes on everything *(reopens the instant-issue-voting question)*

I said "instant direct voting on every issue" is impossible to do well — no human is competent across the whole surface of government ([speed-vs-reversibility §2](speed-vs-reversibility.md)). **César Hidalgo's [Augmented Democracy](https://augmenteddemocracy.com/) attacks exactly that wall:** each citizen has a personalised **AI digital twin** that learns their values and votes on their behalf across *thousands* of issues — instantly, at scale — and the citizen can override it any time. A 2024 study found such agents already predict individual policy choices well above chance.

This is your Question 2's trifecta — **instant + represents the people's will + competent at scale** — which I called impossible *for unaided humans.* With a twin, maybe it isn't. It's [Door 4](../ai-and-power/) turning direct democracy's fatal flaw (nobody can study everything) into a solved engineering problem.

**Frontier risks:** the twin is trained by *someone* — capture moves into the model; does predicting your preference actually *represent your will*, or just your past? manipulation of millions of twins at once; and a system deciding at machine speed is hard to keep reversible.

## Option 4 — Time-boxed absolute power: the Roman-dictator / Cincinnatus model *(this is the honest answer to "isn't speed important?")*

You keep pressing that **speed matters** — and you're right that gridlock is a real failure. Rome's *original* answer (before the word rotted) was a genuine constitutional office: in an emergency a **dictator** was appointed with **absolute power — but for a hard six months, then mandatory surrender of power** ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Cincinnatus/)). Cincinnatus took total command, won in 16 days, and *went back to his farm.* This is **maximum speed with reversibility welded into the clock**: full decisiveness, hard sunset, no re-election, automatic dissolution.

**Frontier risk — and it's the whole game:** the auto-dissolution is *exactly* what modern strongmen break (emergency powers that quietly never end — see [#14](../../problems/14-democratic-backsliding/)). So this option lives or dies on one question: can the sunset be made **truly automatic and self-enforcing** (no human has to *choose* to end it)? If yes, you get the owner's speed safely. If no, it's just a dictatorship with a fuse someone can cut.

## Option 5 — Panarchy: government as a subscription you can switch *(maximal exit)*

[Paul-Émile de Puydt, 1860](http://www.panarchy.org/depuydt/1860.eng.html): **separate government from territory.** You *choose* your government like a service and switch without moving; governments **compete for citizens** as businesses compete for customers; multiple overlapping non-territorial states share the same land. The modern cousin is Frey & Eichenberger's **FOCJ** (functional, overlapping, competing jurisdictions). This is [Door 3 / exit](../country-and-citizenship/) pushed to its limit — accountability by *competition* instead of voice.

**Frontier risks:** genuinely territorial goods (defence, environment, contagion) resist it; it could sort people into sealed ideological bubbles; the mobile and rich exit first, stranding everyone else.

---

## Option 6 — Outcome-triggered auto-dissolution: a skin-in-the-game constitution

Make "judge government on **outcomes**, not procedures" ([Beyond Democracy §6](README.md)) *self-enforcing*: pre-commit measurable thresholds (real income of the bottom half, child mortality, press-freedom) and if a government **misses them, it dissolves automatically** — no election needed, no incumbent's permission. Power that **carries its own downside** ([skin in the game](frontier-mechanisms.md)).

**Frontier risks:** Goodhart (governments optimise the tripwire metric and neglect the rest); whoever sets the threshold holds power; gameable measurement is the same capture surface, again.

---

## How to read this list

These are **not all good ideas** — Options 1 and 3 may be civilisation-level dangerous, and 5 may be unworkable. The point is the opposite of the safe menu: **these are the moves with real upside *and* real downside**, the ones a project that only ever lands on "sortition + reversibility" never lets itself say out loud. The honest work now is to **pick one and build it into a full proposal with a transition path** — not to retreat to the comfortable answer by default.

**Open invitation (owner's call):** which one do we develop — the machine sovereign (1), full lottocracy (2), or digital twins (3)? Each gets the same treatment the other proposals got: steelman → strongest objection → improved version → what we'd measure → who blocks it.
