# The benevolent dictator and the instant referendum — two ways to ask one question

**Status:** Draft for debate · **Last updated:** 2026-06-30 · Companion to [Beyond Democracy](README.md). Answers two owner follow-up questions: *(1) could a country live better under a benevolent dictator than under a slow-moving democracy?* and *(2) is "educated people voting on each issue, instantly" better than a president with high powers?*

> These look like opposite proposals — one concentrates power in a single wise ruler, the other hands every decision straight to the crowd. They are the same bet underneath: **that you get a better country by making decisions faster and more concentrated, with fewer brakes.** This page tests that bet against the evidence. Both proposals fail for the same reason, and the reason tells us what to build instead.

## 0. The question underneath both

Karl Popper reframed the whole problem of government, and his reframe decides both questions. The old question — the one both proposals quietly assume — is **"who should rule?"** (the wisest man? the people directly?). Popper's claim: that is the wrong question, because no selection method reliably finds the right ruler or the right answer. The question that *can* be answered is **"how do we build the state so that bad rulers and bad decisions can be removed without bloodshed?"** Democracy's value is not that the majority is usually right — Popper says plainly it often is not — only that *"this imperfect procedure is the best so far invented"* for getting rid of bad rule cheaply. ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Popper](https://iep.utm.edu/popp-pol/))

That gives the test for both proposals: **not "does it decide faster or more purely?" but "when it gets the decision or the ruler wrong — and it will — how expensive is it to reverse?"** Speed and concentration always improve the *best* case. They are paid for by destroying the *reverse gear*. And the reverse gear is the part that was saving lives.

## 1. Question 1 — the benevolent dictator

Your own instinct already lands on "don't," for the right reason (he could turn on us). The correction is that the case against is **much stronger than a tail risk you are nobly choosing to avoid** — the upside you are giving up is mostly not real, and the comparison with Trump cuts the other way.

**The upside is mostly an illusion.** The reason people believe in benevolent dictators is the high *variance* of autocratic outcomes — "look how fast Singapore / South Korea / China grew." William Easterly took that evidence apart ([*Benevolent Autocrats*, 2011](https://www.nviegi.net/teaching/east1.pdf); [EconTalk interview](https://www.econtalk.org/easterly-on-benevolent-autocrats-and-growth/)): most of that variance is **noise within a single ruler's term, not differences between rulers**, and when autocrats change by chance (death in office), growth barely moves. So the "benevolent autocrat" is a story told *after* the fact — we remember Lee Kuan Yew and forget the far longer list of Mobutu, Mugabe, Pol Pot, Ceaușescu. You are not choosing a fast lane; you are drawing one card from a deck that is mostly disasters, and **you cannot see the cards before you draw.** On expected value the bet loses before the tail risk is even counted.

**Two problems the "benevolent dictator" framing skips entirely:**

| Problem | What it means | Why a democracy's "slowness" already handles it |
|---|---|---|
| **Selection** | You cannot *install* a benevolent dictator. The process that puts dictators in power (force, ruthlessness, purges) selects *against* benevolence. The good ones are accidents. | Elections are an explicit, repeatable selection-and-removal machine that does not require luck. |
| **Succession** | A benevolent dictator is mortal. The next one is a coin-flip with no mechanism behind it. Marcus Aurelius — last of Rome's "good emperors" — handed power to his son Commodus, and the run ended. | The "slow" election cycle *is* the succession mechanism: bloodless, scheduled, by default. |
| **No feedback** | Power with no removal pressure stops getting corrected. "Benevolent" is not a fixed trait; it decays exactly because nothing can punish its decay. | Free press + removability are a continuous error signal the ruler cannot switch off. |

**A fair objection you raised: "doesn't democracy *also* select badly? Decent people are too disgusted by the political 'dogs' to run, so we choose among the power-hungry."** Yes — and it's real and documented. Elections screen *for* whoever can stomach fundraising, public humiliation, and tribal combat, and screen *out* many capable, decent people. Democracy's filter is not clean. But two things rescue the comparison: **(1)** the two filters select for *different* vices — democracy rewards *"can persuade a majority"* (which still punishes openly hating most of the public), autocracy rewards *"can out-violence rivals"*; on the dimension that produces famines and purges (willingness to use force on your own people) the democratic filter is far milder — and it isn't purely bad: democracies are **~20% more likely than autocracies to pick highly-educated leaders** ([Besley & Reynal-Querol, APSR 2011](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/do-democracies-select-more-educated-leaders/E42E837F958ADD92DD3F238523E4F2A0)). **(2)** The deeper point cuts both ways at once: if *no* system reliably selects good leaders — and the impossibility results in [frontier-mechanisms §1](frontier-mechanisms.md) say none can — then the answer is to **stop relying on selection at all.** That is exactly what **sortition** does: you are *drafted* like a juror, so the "decent people who'd never run" are precisely the ones it reaches. Your objection is one of the strongest arguments *for* the sortition mechanisms this project favours — and against pinning hope on electing the right person.

**Now your other sharp point — "a leader can turn on us under democracy too (Trump), so the comparison is not as lopsided as the textbook says."** You are right about the premise and it deserves a straight answer, not a dodge: **"you can just vote them out" is not automatic.** A would-be strongman can be *elected* and then work to disable the machinery that removes him. That is real, it is happening, and it is the entire subject of [#14 Democratic backsliding](../../problems/14-democratic-backsliding/) and the [defending-democracy](../defending-democracy/) proposal — both already in this repo, both sourced to Freedom House / V-Dem / Bright Line Watch.

But the premise being true does **not** flatten the comparison, because the difference was never "democracy never produces a bad leader." The difference Popper named is **recourse and reversibility:**

- When a *democratic* leader turns bad, the removal cost is an election, a court case, a press exposé, a federalism stand-off, a term limit — **bounded, non-violent, and the damage is mostly reversible because the guardrails are still standing.** The U.S. case is evidence of those guardrails being *used* (the 2020 result held; courts heard the challenges; the term limit exists) *and* a warning that they can be strained.
- When a *dictator* turns bad, the only removal mechanism is **death, coup, or revolution** — unbounded, violent, and the damage is often irreversible because he spent his benevolent years dismantling every guardrail.

So Trump is not an argument *for* the dictator. It is an argument that democracy's insurance is **not self-maintaining** and must be hardened — which is a reason to build [defending-democracy](../defending-democracy/), not to hand the keys to someone who would arrive with no guardrails at all. **You were looking for a better driver. The data says safety never came from the driver — it came from the brakes and the reverse gear.**

**Verdict:** No. Not mainly because of the tail risk you flagged, but because the upside is survivorship bias (Easterly), the selection and succession problems have no solution, and the one thing a dictator structurally removes — cheap, non-violent reversibility — is the exact thing that prevents famines, purges, and looting ([Beyond Democracy §2](README.md): no famine in a functioning democracy; ~4× lower growth volatility).

## 2. Question 2 — educated people voting on every issue, instantly

This proposal bundles three claims. Separate them, because they are not equally good: **(a) restrict the vote to the educated**, **(b) vote directly on each issue**, **(c) decide instantly.** One is reaching for something real. Two are the same brake-removal mistake as Question 1.

**(a) "Educated people" — this is epistocracy, and this project already examined and rejected it.** See [political-system §4](../political-system/) and the handover record: weighting or restricting votes by education correlates with wealth and race (it entrenches the already-powerful in a feedback loop — Brennan's own "demographic objection"); it is *literally* the mechanism Jim Crow literacy tests used to disenfranchise Black voters; and whoever gets to define "educated" controls the electorate. The qualifier re-opens a grave we already filled. Drop it. (There is a defensible version of "let the people who know decide" — it is delegation, not a franchise test. See below.)

**(b)+(c) "Vote directly on each issue, instantly" — the instant part is the bug, not the feature.** Two hard problems:

- **Competence does not scale across the whole surface of governance.** No one — however educated — is competent on monetary policy *and* nuclear-reactor safety *and* antibiotic stewardship *and* tax incidence *and* semiconductor export control. An educated biologist is a layman on interest rates. "Educated" does not fix this; the cost of being genuinely informed on *every* issue is prohibitive, so instant issue-votes get decided on slogans, not knowledge.
- **An instant majority with no deliberation and no rights floor strips minorities immediately — this is not hypothetical.** California **Proposition 8**: 52% of voters banned same-sex marriage by direct ballot (2008); it was undone only by a *court*, years later ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_California_Proposition_8)). The **Swiss minaret ban**: 57.5% voted to forbid a religious minority's architecture (2009), condemned by the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights as *"discriminatory"* ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2009/12/swiss-law-banning-minarets-clearly-discriminatory-un-human-rights-chief); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Swiss_minaret_referendum)). In **both** cases the only thing that protected the minority was a *counter-majoritarian* brake — a court, an international rights body — i.e. the "slow," "undemocratic" institution the instant-vote model is built to bypass. James Madison made exactly this argument in [Federalist No. 10](https://guides.loc.gov/federalist-papers/text-1-10#s-lg-box-wrapper-25493273): a pure direct democracy has no cure for a momentary faction; a republic deliberately filters and slows it. **"Instant" is precisely what turns a transient majority mood into permanent damage.**

**What your instinct gets right — and it is important.** Direct issue-voting beats a powerful president on one decisive axis: **it cannot be captured the way a representative can.** There is no middleman to buy. That is a direct strike at the [Gilens-Page](README.md) problem — the captured proxy this whole proposal is about. You are reaching for something genuine: *cut out the buyable representative.* The task is to keep that capture-resistance **without** paying in incompetence and trampled minorities.

**The version that keeps the good and drops the failure modes** (this is [Beyond Democracy §4](README.md), now sharpened by your two questions):

- **Sortition + deliberation (citizens' assemblies).** A random, representative sample of citizens, given *time* and *expert testimony*, deliberates and then decides. This keeps "the people's will" — statistically it *is* the people — adds the competence a raw referendum lacks, and **cannot be lobbied** (you can't buy a panel you can't predict). It converts your "instant" (the bug) into "deliberated" (the feature). Ireland is the worked case: random assemblies broke decades of deadlock and led to the 2015 and 2018 referendums ([§4](README.md)).
- **Liquid democracy / revocable delegation.** Vote directly on what you know; **delegate your vote — revocably, topic by topic — to someone you trust** on what you don't. *This* is the honest, defensible version of "let the educated decide": you delegate to the expert **by your own choice**, with no state official defining "educated" and no one disenfranchised. [political-system](../political-system/) says outright this is what your founding idea was really reaching for.
- **A rights floor + courts.** The counter-majoritarian brake that would have stopped Prop 8 and the minaret ban *at the door*. Non-negotiable — it is the reversibility the instant referendum throws away.

**Verdict:** "Educated people voting on each issue, instantly" beats a powerful president on capture-resistance (real, keep it) but loses on two counts it shares with the dictator: the "educated" filter is epistocracy (disenfranchising, capturable), and "instant" removes the deliberation and the rights brake that stop majorities from doing irreversible harm. Replace *educated filter* → *voluntary delegation*, and *instant* → *deliberation + rights floor*, and you get what you actually want. The powerful-president alternative, meanwhile, is just Question 1 wearing a sash.

## 3. What unifies the two answers

Both proposals try to buy a better outcome with **speed and concentration**. Both pay for it by removing **reversibility** — the dictator removes it on the *ruler* axis (you can't vote him out), the instant referendum removes it on the *decision* axis (50%+1 becomes law with no appeal). And reversibility is not a side-feature of democracy that we could trim to go faster. **The slowness *is* the error-correction.** They are the same mechanism; you cannot keep one and cut the other.

The verified picture says the brakes are the part that works: no famine in a functioning democracy, ~4× lower growth volatility ([§2](README.md)), majorities reliably wrong often enough that Prop 8 and the minaret ban needed courts to undo them. So the project's direction is right and these two questions sharpen *why*: **optimize for reversibility, not for speed.** Every mechanism in [Beyond Democracy §4](README.md) — sortition, liquid delegation, money-out, free press, a rights floor — keeps decisions reversible and rulers removable *while* attacking the capture you correctly hate. That is the whole trick: get the capture-resistance you were chasing in both questions, without throwing away the reverse gear.

## 4. "But isn't speed important? Humanity barely evolved politically in 2,000 years."

Fair challenge — and the framing above was one-sided. **Speed does matter:** a system that *can't act* (gridlock, sclerosis, no response to a crisis) is also a failure. So the rule is not "slow good, fast bad." It is: **reversibility is what *licenses* speed.** If a decision is cheap to undo, you can afford to move fast and experiment on it; if it's irreversible (rights, war, constitutional structure), you slow down. The two aren't opposites — *reversibility is the thing that makes speed safe.* The buildable version is **reversibility-grading**: fast-track easily-undone decisions, put the high bar only on the one-way doors ([frontier-mechanisms §2](frontier-mechanisms.md)). Both of your proposals fail not because they're fast, but because they make the *irreversible* things fast.

**But "humanity barely evolved politically in 2,000 years" is simply false — and it's the most important error to fix.** Politically, the last ~250 years are the **fastest and deepest transformation in human history** — it only looks like "nothing changed" because we live inside the result and treat it as normal:

- **Slavery** — universal and unquestioned for ~5,000 years — was **legally abolished almost everywhere in the 1800s.**
- **Absolute monarchy / divine right**, the default operating system of states, was **overthrown as the norm.**
- **Universal suffrage** is ~100 years old; before that, in all of history, **zero women voted anywhere**, and the U.S. only reached it with the [1965 Voting Rights Act](frontier-mechanisms.md).
- **Decolonization** turned dozens of imperial subjects into self-governing states in a single mid-20th-century generation.
- The very *ideas* that rulers are removable, that government needs consent, that rights are universal — **all new.**

A visitor from 1700 would find our political world unrecognizable; a visitor from 100 CE would think it magic. And here is the sting for the "speed" argument: **that explosion of political progress was delivered by the slow, reversible, deliberative system** — not by the fast, concentrated ones. The slow system out-evolved every empire and autocracy *because* it could try things, keep what worked, and undo what didn't. Reversibility didn't block political evolution; it's what made rapid evolution survivable.

**Your frustration is still valid — but it's aimed one era too wide.** We made enormous progress and then **stalled, recently, on one specific thing: capture** (the [money→power loop, #01→#05](../../problems/01-economic-inequality/)). That stall is this entire project's subject. And the bottleneck there is **not decision-speed** — it's the **transition problem** ([proposal 00](../00-how-change-happens/)): entrenched power resisting change. A faster decision mechanism doesn't dissolve that resistance; it just lets *whoever holds the fast mechanism* impose without the others' consent — which is the dictator/instant-vote trap again. The cure for a stall caused by capture is **de-capture**, not acceleration.
