# Proposal · Founding a Country — How a New Polity Would Be Led

**Status:** Draft for debate · **Last updated:** 2026-06-29 · **Door 3** of [proposal 00](../00-how-change-happens/) (exit and build new), and the place to actually *implement* the good parts of the [political-system proposal](../political-system/) without incumbents blocking them. Relates to [#05 Governance](../../problems/05-governance-democracy/), [#12 Rights & justice](../../problems/12-rights-justice/), and — as the track record shows — [#14 Backsliding](../../problems/14-democratic-backsliding/).

> Added at the owner's request: *"Country and citizenship — how a possible new country would be led."* (This replaces the empty, mis-named top-level folder; moved here under `proposals/` and renamed to repo kebab-case convention.)

## Why this door exists

If a captured system can't be reformed from within (Door 1 is guarded) and you don't want to wait for it to collapse, the remaining move is to **build a clean polity and let people choose it.** The appeal is real and specific: *there are no incumbents to defeat.* You can write the rules right on day one — direct election, a constitutional rights floor, sortition assemblies, money-out-of-politics — instead of fighting the people who profit from the old rules. This is the one door where the project's ideal designs could actually be tried from scratch.

That is the promise. The track record below is why this folder is skeptical of it.

## The design space (with what each really is)

- **Charter cities / special jurisdictions** — a host country grants a zone its own legal, fiscal, and administrative rules. The flagship case is **Próspera**, on Roatán, Honduras, created under the Honduran "ZEDE" framework (constitutional amendments, 2012–13).
- **Network states** (Balaji Srinivasan) — defined as *"a highly aligned online community with a capacity for collective action that crowdfunds territory around the world and eventually gains diplomatic recognition from pre-existing states."* It starts online with an agreed leader, an integrated cryptocurrency, and a moral purpose, then crowdfunds a non-contiguous *archipelago* of parcels nested inside existing law (nonprofit or special-zone tenant), and only seeks sovereignty after reaching scale. More realistic than seasteading because it starts *inside* the law — but see objection #3: an "agreed-upon leader" plus a built-in token is a capture structure by design, and the early members are the already-aligned and already-capitalized.
- **Seasteading / new physical territory** — sovereignty on the ocean or unclaimed land. Mostly blocked by physics, defense, and the law of the sea.
- **Micronations** — almost always unrecognized; useful only as thought experiments.

## The track record — this door has no success stories yet

The honest headline from the development literature: **charter cities "remain an idea without any success stories."** *(Rethink Priorities; Devpolicy.)* Two cases show *why*, and both point straight back at the objections below:

- **Próspera v. Honduras — the escape-pod objection, made real.** Honduras's voters elected a president on a platform of repealing the ZEDE framework; in April 2022 Congress repealed it **128–0** (i.e. across all parties), and in 2024 the Supreme Court declared the framework unconstitutional *ab initio*. Próspera's response was not to accept the democratic verdict of the country it sits in — it filed an **international investor-arbitration claim (ICSID) for up to US$10.7 billion, since revised toward US$26.4 billion**, a sum that would be ruinous for one of the hemisphere's poorer economies. Honduras moved to **withdraw from ICSID** rather than be tried there; in Feb 2025 the tribunal let the case proceed. *(UNCTAD case file; Kluwer Arbitration Blog.)* This is the danger of objection #1 in concrete form: the dispute is now resolved **outside** Honduran democracy, in a venue accountable to investors — accountability drained from the place people actually live.
- **Romer's charter cities — sovereignty is conceded, not granted.** Economist Paul Romer secured a Madagascar president's backing for two charter cities in 2008; **within months that president was deposed in a coup** and the project was branded treason and shelved. In Honduras, when the Supreme Court first struck down the enabling law in 2012, **the judges who had ruled against it were removed from the court.** *(Jacobin; Wikipedia, charter-city history.)* Note the second case carefully: the charter-city push did not just *fail* — it **triggered a democratic-backsliding event** (purging judges) in the host. Door 3 can corrode Door 2.

## The honest objections (this door is dangerous too)

1. **The rich-escape-pod problem.** A new polity that mainly lets capital flee taxation and accountability doesn't fix [#01](../../problems/01-economic-inequality/) — it *is* #01, with a flag. The Próspera arbitration is this objection happening: who is **excluded**, and does the arrangement **drain accountability** from the democracy hosting it?
2. **Sovereignty is conceded, not granted.** Existing states rarely give up real authority; "your own rules" and even a written "50-year stability guarantee" last exactly until voters or a coup withdraw them — as both cases above demonstrate.
3. **Who are the founders accountable to?** A polity designed by a founder and funded by investors (or token-holders) can reproduce the capture problem in a smaller, *less escapable* form. Day-one rules written by whoever paid for the land.
4. **Legitimacy and defense.** A polity that can't defend itself or win recognition isn't a country; it's a business park with aspirations — and the host can revoke it.
5. **Exit can weaken voice.** Every capable person who exits a struggling democracy is one fewer person fixing it (Door 1/2). Door 3 can quietly cannibalize the others (Hirschman's *exit vs. voice*).

## Does any design survive objection #1?

On the evidence: **the literal "new sovereign country" forms do not — yet.** Every real attempt has either been repealed by the host's democracy (Próspera), erased by a change of regime (Madagascar), or has triggered backsliding to defend itself (the judge purge). That is a strong empirical verdict, not a hunch.

What *might* survive is a narrower claim: keep the **clean-slate design benefit** while dropping the sovereignty fight and the escape-pod incentive. Two salvage routes:

- **Special *democratic* zones inside an existing state** — a city or region that adopts the project's governance rules (direct election, sortition, transparency, a rights floor) under the national constitution, with citizens who *also* remain full national citizens. This keeps voice, avoids the tax-haven incentive, and needs no recognition fight. It is Door 1 wearing Door 3's clothes — and probably the only version that doesn't reproduce #01.
- **The design exercise as a reusable artifact** (below) — valuable even if no territory is ever acquired.

## The genuinely interesting question — the reusable artifact

Set aside whether a *new* country is feasible. The design exercise is valuable regardless: **if you could write a constitution from scratch, knowing everything in this repo, what would you refuse to repeat?** That output — a charter that bakes in money-out-of-politics, insulated referees ([#14](../../problems/14-democratic-backsliding/)), a compute/AI-power clause ([ai-and-power](../ai-and-power/)), and an information-integrity floor ([information-integrity](../information-integrity/)) — is reusable. It is the *spec* that Doors 1 and 2 are trying to reach anyway. So this folder earns its place even if no new country is ever founded; its real product is the spec, not the soil.

## What we'd measure

- Does the proposed citizenship rule **include or exclude** the people who most need better governance?
- Capture resistance: can the founder/funders/token-holders bend the rules to entrench themselves? (Stress-test against the political-system proposal's own §7 criteria.)
- Does it **add** accountable governance to the world, or just **subtract** it from existing states (the Próspera test)?

## Transition path (who blocks this · which door · smallest first move)

- **Who blocks it:** host governments (they lose tax base and authority — and their *voters* may repeal the deal, as Honduras did); and the project's own values, which reject an escape pod for the rich.
- **Which door:** this *is* Door 3 — but the evidence pushes the workable version back toward **Door 1** (a special democratic zone inside an existing constitution) rather than new sovereignty.
- **Smallest first move:** **write the constitution, not the country.** Produce the "what would we refuse to repeat?" charter as a public document and pitch it to an existing sub-national government (a willing city/region) as an opt-in governance upgrade — clean-slate rules, no new border, no tax escape, citizens keep their voice. If even *that* can't find a taker, the lesson is that the obstacle was never the rules — it was always the transition, which sends us back to the spine.

## Open questions

- Is there any literal new-country design that survives objection #1 — or is the special-democratic-zone the honest ceiling for this door?
- What is the minimum viable population / territory / recognition for legitimacy, and is it reachable without becoming a tax haven first?
- Can the "network state" model reach recognition *without* its leader-plus-token structure reproducing the capture problem it was meant to escape?
- Does a clean-slate charter, once written, actually pull Doors 1 and 2 toward it — or does it just sit on a shelf?
