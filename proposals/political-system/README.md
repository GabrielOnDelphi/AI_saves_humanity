# Proposal · A New Kind of Democracy: Direct Election, Education-Weighted Voting

**Status:** Draft for debate · **Last updated:** 2026-06-16 · Relates to [problem #05 Governance](../../problems/05-governance-democracy/), [#03 Education](../../problems/03-education/), [#12 Rights & Justice](../../problems/12-rights-justice/)

> This is the founding proposal of the project. It is written the way every proposal here should be: **the idea stated fairly first, then stress-tested without mercy.** The goal is not to win the argument for or against — it's to find the version that actually survives.

> **Deeper reframe (read alongside this):** [`beyond-democracy`](../beyond-democracy/) argues the founder's instinct — that conventional democracy underperforms — is *right*, but aimed one layer too high. The real target isn't *who votes* (this proposal's question) but *who captures the outcome* ([Gilens-Page](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/testing-theories-of-american-politics-elites-interest-groups-and-average-citizens/62327F513959D0A304D4893B382B992B): elites, not voters, drive policy). It relocates the goal from "better democracy" to "accountable power + flourishing," which sortition and liquid delegation reach without the dangers of vote-weighting below.

> **Owner pushback + improved version (2026-06-30):** the owner challenged the quick dismissal of (B) — *"why drop it because one critic said so? Every system failed at least once."* — and proposed a **self-liquidating** design: educate the excluded → their vote weighs more → they vote for more education → the gap closes as schooling becomes universal. That improved version is taken seriously and stress-tested (by structure, not authority) in [`epistocracy-improved.md`](epistocracy-improved.md). Verdict: **the goal and the positive loop are good; vote-weighting is the wrong engine** — it inverts the incentives and hands whoever defines "educated" a dial to capture the electorate. Keep the loop; run it on equal votes + deliberation.

## 1. The proposal, as stated

> *Every single person votes directly for the head of state (the president). There is no electoral college and no parliament choosing the leader on the people's behalf — it is one direct, national vote. Each person's vote is **weighted by their level of education**: a more-educated voter's ballot counts for more.*

The intuition behind it is serious and worth respecting: **decisions made by better-informed people tend to be better decisions.** Many elections are decided by voters who haven't studied the issues, are swayed by slogans, or are actively manipulated (see [#10 Information](../../problems/10-information-truth/)). If we let the people most equipped to evaluate policy carry more weight, the theory goes, we'd get wiser leaders and better government — and we'd reward the effort of becoming educated.

There are really **two distinct ideas** bundled here, and they should be judged separately:

- **(A) Direct election of the head of state** — every citizen votes directly, no intermediaries.
- **(B) Education-weighted votes** — ballots count differently based on the voter's education.

(A) is mainstream and defensible. (B) is the radical, dangerous part. Most of this document is about (B), because that's where the project has to be honest.

---

## 2. Where this idea comes from (intellectual lineage)

This is not a new thought, and pretending it is would be dishonest. It has names and a long history:

- **Plural voting / weighted suffrage** — giving some citizens more votes than others.
- **Epistocracy** — literally "rule by the knowledgeable"; the broader family of "let competence shape political power."
- **John Stuart Mill**, *Considerations on Representative Government* (1861), explicitly proposed **extra votes for the more educated and skilled** — while insisting it must *not* be tied to wealth, and that universal suffrage must come first. He saw it as a temporary corrective, not a caste system.
- **Jason Brennan**, *Against Democracy* (2016), is the best modern defense of epistocracy — and, crucially, he also names its strongest objection (see below).
- On the other side stands the **one-person-one-vote** tradition: political equality as a moral baseline, won through centuries of struggle.

Knowing the lineage matters because the idea has already been *tried* in various forms — and we can look at what happened.

## 3. The honest case for it (steelman)

If we're going to attack the idea, we first state its strongest form:

1. **Voter ignorance is real and measurable.** Large fractions of electorates can't name basic facts about policy or government. "Rational ignorance" — the rational choice not to study issues when your single vote almost never decides anything — is a genuine structural flaw in mass democracy.
2. **Many decisions have real technical content.** Monetary policy, pandemic response, and climate strategy have right-er and wrong-er answers that expertise helps identify.
3. **We already accept competence gates elsewhere.** We require licenses to fly planes or practice medicine. Why is steering an entire nation the one high-stakes activity with zero competence requirement?
4. **It rewards effort.** Tying influence to education could motivate people to become informed.

This is not a stupid idea. It is a serious answer to a real problem (the voter-information root cause in [#05](../../problems/05-governance-democracy/)). The question is whether the *cure* is worse than the disease.

---

## 4. The case against it (the pushback)

Here is where I'll be direct, because the project's rules demand it: **as literally specified — weighting votes by formal education — this proposal is dangerous, and I'd advise against shipping it as the headline reform.** The reasons:

1. **It violates political equality — the moral core of democracy.** "Some citizens' voices count less" is precisely what democracy was built to abolish. A janitor and a professor have equal stakes in whether the country goes to war, taxes their wages, or drafts their children. Equal stakes, equal say.

2. **Education ≠ wisdom, and most political questions are about values, not facts.** Whether to prioritize liberty over equality, how to weigh future generations against present ones, what we owe the poor — these are moral and value questions on which a chemistry PhD has **no special authority**. Expertise answers "how," not "what we should want." Weighting votes by education smuggles in the false claim that the educated are morally wiser, not just more credentialed.

3. **Education correlates with wealth, race, and geography — so this entrenches privilege and creates a feedback loop.** The already-advantaged get more education, so they'd get more votes, so they'd shape the rules (including *who gets education*) to favor themselves — who then get more votes. This is the **money→power→money loop of [#01](../../problems/01-economic-inequality/) and [#05](../../problems/05-governance-democracy/) with a constitutional turbocharger.** Brennan, epistocracy's own defender, calls this the **demographic objection** and considers it the strongest argument against his own position: if the enfranchised/empowered group is demographically skewed, the system will systematically serve that group's interests.

4. **History is damning. This exact mechanism was the tool of disenfranchisement.** Literacy tests, "understanding clauses," and education/property qualifications were *precisely* how the U.S. Jim Crow South stripped Black citizens of the vote; how women, the poor, and colonized peoples were excluded for centuries. "Weight the vote by education" is not a novel reform — it is the historical machinery of exclusion, wearing a meritocratic mask. Any honest version of this proposal must reckon with the fact that it has been tried, and it was used for evil.

5. **Whoever defines "education level" controls the electorate.** Diplomas? A state-administered test? A government body? This is a catastrophic capture surface (see [#05](../../problems/05-governance-democracy/)): an incumbent can redefine "qualifying education" to inflate its own base and deflate its opponents'. The metric *is* the power.

6. **It would shred legitimacy and invite unrest.** A system that openly tells a large share of the population "your vote weighs less" gives them little reason to accept its outcomes — the recipe for instability and, eventually, violence ([#09](../../problems/09-conflict-peace/)).

7. **Goodhart's law.** Once schooling buys political power, credentials get gamed and bought, and "education" stops measuring what we wanted it to.

**Summary of the critique:** the *goal* — better-informed collective decisions — is excellent. But (B), education-weighted voting, achieves it by recreating the precise instrument history used for disenfranchisement, while building a privilege feedback loop and a capture surface into the constitution. The defensible kernel deserves a better delivery system.

## 5. Keep the goal, change the mechanism (improved / alternative versions)

The right move is to **separate the goal from this particular instrument** and find designs that raise decision quality *without* taking the vote away from anyone. These are the alternatives the project should test against the original:

### Tier 1 — Raise competence without touching vote weight (recommended starting point)

- **Deliberative democracy + sortition.** Randomly selected citizens' assemblies (like a jury, but for policy) study an issue deeply with expert input, then decide or recommend. This captures the *informed-decision* benefit while preserving equality, because everyone has an equal chance of serving and the body mirrors the population. It has a real, growing track record (Ireland's assemblies on hard constitutional questions; OECD has catalogued hundreds of cases).
- **Liquid democracy (delegated voting).** Everyone keeps an equal vote, but may *voluntarily* delegate it, per-topic, to someone they trust who's more informed — and revoke that delegation any time. You get expertise-weighting **by consent**, not by decree.
- **Universal high-quality civic education + deliberation days + voter information.** Attack the ignorance root directly (see [#03](../../problems/03-education/)) instead of disenfranchising its victims.

### Tier 2 — If you insist on a knowledge filter, make it equal-access and a floor, not a multiplier

- A **civic-knowledge component** that is (a) about *issue/civic knowledge*, not formal schooling; (b) **free and universally available to everyone** so it doesn't track wealth or class; and (c) a low **eligibility floor** anyone can clear with effort — *not* a multiplier that stacks power on the already-powerful.
- ⚠️ **Honesty note:** even this still has the disenfranchisement problem and the ugly historical baggage of literacy tests. It is *less* bad than weighting by diplomas, but it is not clean. The project should treat it as a red-flag option, not a recommendation.

### Tier 3 — Structural safeguards that make *any* of the above safer

- **Direct election (idea A) — yes.** Electing the head of state directly is defensible and worth keeping, ideally with a majority-runoff or ranked-choice ballot so the winner has real majority support.
- **Constitutional rights floor.** Whatever the voting rule, fundamental rights ([#12](../../problems/12-rights-justice/)) are placed beyond the reach of any electorate — weighted or not — so no majority (or empowered minority) can strip them.
- **Separate "values" from "means."** Citizens decide *direction* (one person, one vote); expert agencies execute the *technical means* under that democratic mandate and accountability. This is roughly how the best democratic-technocratic hybrids already work — and it captures most of the real benefit epistocracy is reaching for, with none of the disenfranchisement.

---

## 6. Recommendation

- **Adopt (A):** direct election of the head of state, with a majority-securing ballot (runoff or ranked-choice). Defensible, modern, keeps everyone equal.
- **Do not adopt (B) as specified.** Education-weighted voting reproduces the historical machinery of disenfranchisement, builds a privilege feedback loop, and hands whoever defines "education" control of the electorate.
- **Pursue the real goal — better-informed decisions — through Tier 1:** sortition-based citizens' assemblies and voluntary liquid-democracy delegation, backed by universal civic education and a constitutional rights floor.

This keeps the founder's actual aim — *wiser collective decisions* — and drops only the part that history and logic say will backfire. That is exactly the kind of trade the project exists to find.

---

## 7. What we'd measure (to know if a redesign actually works)

- **Decision quality:** do choices made under the new system look better in hindsight (outcomes, expert post-mortems) than under plain majority vote?
- **Equality preserved:** does any group end up systematically under-represented? (The demographic objection, made into a live metric.)
- **Legitimacy:** do losers accept outcomes? Turnout, trust, and unrest.
- **Capture resistance:** can an incumbent bend the rules to entrench itself? Stress-test every design against this.
- **Informedness:** measured civic knowledge of the deciding body vs. the general population.

---

## 8. Open questions

- Is there *any* version of competence-weighting that escapes the demographic objection? (Brennan himself is unsure. The project's working answer: not safely — prefer deliberation and delegation.)
- Can deliberative assemblies be given *binding* power without losing the legitimacy that makes them work?
- For direct national election (A), how do you prevent pure populism / demagoguery without disenfranchising anyone? (Part of the answer is [#03](../../problems/03-education/) and [#10](../../problems/10-information-truth/).)
- How do you transition from a captured status quo ([#05](../../problems/05-governance-democracy/)) to *any* of these without the incumbents blocking it?

## 9. Sources & further reading

- John Stuart Mill, *Considerations on Representative Government* (1861) — the original plural-voting argument.
- Jason Brennan, *Against Democracy* (2016) — modern defense of epistocracy **and** its strongest self-criticism (the demographic objection).
- David Estlund, *Democratic Authority* (2008) — a careful argument for why epistocracy fails to justify itself.
- Hélène Landemore, *Democratic Reason* (2013) and *Open Democracy* (2020) — the case that inclusive, deliberative democracy is *epistemically* superior to rule by the few.
- OECD, *Innovative Citizen Participation and New Democratic Institutions* ("the deliberative wave") — evidence base for sortition/assemblies: https://www.oecd.org/gov/innovative-citizen-participation.htm
- On the U.S. history of literacy tests as disenfranchisement tools — see the historical record of Jim Crow voting laws and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

*If you want to defend education-weighted voting, the most useful contribution you can make is a design that survives objection #3 (the demographic/feedback-loop objection) and objection #4 (the historical track record). If it can't, it shouldn't ship.*
