# 09 · Conflict, War & Peace

**Status:** Draft · **Last updated:** 2026-06-16

## Scope

Organized violence between and within states, and the conditions that prevent it. In scope: interstate war, civil war, great-power rivalry, the arms trade, and peacebuilding. War is a **threat multiplier** — it destroys health systems (#02), drives hunger (#07), halts education (#03), and raises catastrophic risk (#06).

## Key facts

- The number of active **state-based armed conflicts is at its highest since 1945**, and the trend over the past decade is upward. *(Uppsala Conflict Data Program; PRIO)*
- Conflict is now the **leading driver of acute food crises** — most people facing famine-level hunger are in war zones (link to #07).
- Wars create the world's **displacement crisis**: well over 100 million people are forcibly displaced — the highest figure on record. *(UNHCR)*
- Global **military spending is at record highs**, money diverted from health, education, and climate (links to #01, #02, #03).
- **Great-power tension** is rising, which simultaneously raises nuclear and AI/bio arms-race risk (link to #06).

## Root causes

- **Insecurity and the security dilemma.** States arm to feel safe; their arming makes rivals feel unsafe — a self-fueling spiral.
- **Resources and grievance.** Competition over land, water, oil, and minerals (worsened by #04), plus inequality and exclusion (#01, #12), light and sustain conflicts.
- **Weak or captured governance.** Fragile states, contested legitimacy, and corruption (#05) make internal war more likely.
- **Profit and the arms trade.** A large industry benefits from weapons demand.
- **Eroding global institutions.** A weakening international order leaves fewer brakes on the use of force.

## Connections

- **→ #07 Food / #02 Health / #03 Education:** war destroys the systems that deliver them.
- **↔ #06 Existential risk:** great-power conflict is the main path to nuclear use and arms races.
- **← #04 Climate & #01 Inequality:** scarcity and grievance feed conflict.
- **→ #11 Mental health & #12 Rights:** trauma, refugees, atrocities.

## Candidate solutions (with trade-offs)

1. **Strengthen diplomacy, mediation & international law.** *For:* prevention is far cheaper than war. *Objection:* depends on great-power consent exactly where it's weakest.
2. **Arms control & de-escalation** (see #06). *For:* lowers the worst-case ceiling. *Objection:* requires trust between rivals; verification is hard.
3. **Address root grievances** — inclusion, fair resource-sharing, anti-corruption (#01, #05, #12). *For:* tackles causes, not symptoms. *Objection:* slow; outsiders can't impose it.
4. **Peacekeeping & post-conflict reconstruction.** *For:* can hold fragile peace and rebuild. *Objection:* mixed record; underfunded; can entrench rather than resolve.
5. **Regulate the arms trade** and conflict financing. *For:* raises the cost of war-making. *Objection:* powerful industry and state resistance; gray markets.

## Metrics we'd watch

- Number and intensity of active conflicts; battle and civilian deaths.
- Forcibly displaced population.
- Global military spending vs. spending on health/education/climate.
- People in conflict-driven food crises.

## Open questions

- How do you restrain great-power conflict when the institutions meant to do so are themselves contested?
- Can climate-driven resource conflict be defused before it scales?
- What actually makes post-conflict peace durable rather than a pause?
