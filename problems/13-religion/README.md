# 13 · Religion, Coercion & Freedom of Conscience

**Status:** Draft · **Last updated:** 2026-06-16 · Relates to [#12 Rights & Justice](../12-rights-justice/), [#09 Conflict](../09-conflict-peace/), [#05 Governance](../05-governance-democracy/), [#03 Education](../03-education/)

> **A framing note up front, because it matters.** Religion is not a "problem" the way hunger or war is. It is double-edged: the same force that drove the Inquisition, religious wars, and the persecution of heretics also founded many of the world's first hospitals, universities, and charities, and gives billions of people community, meaning, and moral structure. So this folder is **not** "religion is bad." The actual problem is narrower and sharper: **religious *coercion* and religiously-justified *harm*** — when belief is forced, fused with state power, or used to deny others their rights. Treating belief itself as the enemy is both factually wrong and a path that has its own body count (see the anti-religious regimes below). Keep that distinction or this folder becomes a prejudice instead of an analysis.
>
> **Two things this folder does *not* claim.** (1) That religion is *true* — it isn't science, its core claims are unfalsifiable, and it gets no epistemic deference here. (2) That it's *good because it's widespread* — popularity is not evidence of truth or merit (that's the *ad populum* fallacy). Prevalence matters only for *tactics*: coercive bans on a belief held by billions backfire and create martyrs. The honest claim is the narrow one — religion's *effects* are empirically mixed (not uniformly bad), and the thing worth acting on is *coercion and harm*, not belief itself.

## Scope

In scope: religiously-motivated violence and persecution; the fusion of religion with state power; religiously-justified denial of rights (to women, LGBT people, apostates, minorities); suppression of science and education on doctrinal grounds; and — symmetrically — the *persecution of believers* and the coercive suppression of religion. Out of scope: the truth or falsehood of any religion's claims (not the project's business) and private, peaceful belief (a protected right, not a problem).

## Key facts

- About **84% of the world's population is religiously affiliated** (Pew, 2010 baseline); the religiously unaffiliated ("nones") rose to **~1.9 billion by 2020**, two-thirds of them in one country (China). The world is *slowly* becoming less religious on average, while Islam is the fastest-growing group. *(Pew, 2025)*
- Religion is **deeply woven into most people's lives and identities** — which is exactly why coercion around it is so explosive, and why heavy-handed "solutions" backfire.
- **Government restrictions on religion are near record highs**, and the number of countries with high/very-high *social hostilities* involving religion rose to **55 in 2023** (from 45 the year before). Among the 25 most populous countries, India, Egypt, Pakistan, Iran, and Indonesia had the highest combined restrictions. *(Pew, 16th annual report, June 2026)*
- **Historical harm is enormous and real:** the European wars of religion (the Thirty Years' War alone killed millions), the Crusades, the Inquisition, witch hunts, the religious dimension of the 1947 Partition of India, sectarian violence, and religiously-framed terrorism.
- **Coercive *anti*-religion is also catastrophic:** the USSR, Maoist China, the Khmer Rouge, and Albania (which in 1967 declared itself the world's first officially atheist state and banned religion outright) persecuted and killed believers en masse. **The lesson: the harm comes from coercion, not from which side coerces.**

## Root causes

- **Fusion of religion and state power.** When a religion controls the law, dissenters and minorities lose their rights. Most religious oppression runs through *state* power, not private belief.
- **Absolute-truth claims meeting political power.** A belief held as the one infallible truth, plus the authority to enforce it, plus an out-group, is a recipe for persecution — and this is true of *secular* absolutisms too.
- **Identity and tribalism.** Religion often marks in-group/out-group lines; conflicts that are really about land, power, or grievance ([#01](../01-economic-inequality/), [#09](../09-conflict-peace/)) get a religious label and become harder to resolve.
- **Doctrine vs. evidence.** Where doctrine is allowed to override evidence in law or schooling, science and rights suffer ([#03](../03-education/)).
- **Manipulation.** Leaders weaponize faith to mobilize, divide, and control — religion is the tool, power is the goal.

## Candidate solutions (with trade-offs)

The defensible core is well established and the project should adopt it:

1. **A secular state (separation of religion and state power).** The government endorses, funds, and enforces *no* religion — and equally, persecutes none. *For:* this is the single highest-leverage fix; it removes the state as a weapon for or against any faith. *Objection:* hard where one religion is fused with national identity; must be neutrality, not state atheism (which is just coercion wearing the other jersey).
2. **The harm standard (your proposed rule, part 1).** Everyone is free to believe and practice, *unless* the practice harms others — incitement to violence, coercion of children, denial of others' rights, forced conversion. *For:* this is essentially the existing international-law standard and it's sound (see below). *Objection:* "harm" must be defined tightly (real, concrete harm to others — not "I'm offended"), or it becomes a tool to ban disfavored faiths.
3. **Protect the vulnerable from coercion** — especially children's right to an evidence-based education and to not be forced into belief; women's and minorities' rights to override religious exemptions that strip them. *For:* targets the actual harm. *Objection:* tension with parents' rights and community autonomy.
4. **Equal treatment + freedom to leave.** No legal penalty for apostasy, conversion, or unbelief; no privileging one faith over another or over none. *For:* freedom of conscience made real. *Objection:* politically explosive where apostasy is criminalized.

### The proposed ground rule — and where I'd push back

> **Your rule, as stated:** *Everyone is free to believe in any religion, as long as its teachings don't promote harm; and — exactly like the planned rule on advertising — religious belief cannot be promoted on public channels or in public places.*

**Part 1 (believe freely, no harm): keep it.** This is almost exactly the standard already written into international human-rights law. ICCPR Article 18 says manifesting religion "may be subject only to such limitations as are prescribed by law and are necessary to protect public safety, order, health, or morals **or the fundamental rights and freedoms of others**." That *is* the harm principle. Defensible and well-founded.

**Part 2 (no promotion in public channels or places): I'd advise against it as written — it conflicts with the very rights this project defends.** Directly:

- **It collides head-on with freedom of expression and private belief.** UDHR/ICCPR Article 18 protects manifesting belief "in public or private" — an adult speaking about their faith in public is exercising a right, and a blanket ban forbids it. **One caveat the project owner is right about:** Article 18's word "teaching" covers a *community instructing its own consenting members* and teaching *about* belief — it is **not** a license to teach doctrine *as established fact*, least of all to children. That confessional, taught-as-truth kind can and should be restricted (see the next subsection). But banning an adult from *expressing or discussing* belief in public still solves a rights problem ([#12](../12-rights-justice/)) by violating a right.
- **The advertising analogy breaks at the key point.** Commercial advertising is not a human right; religious (and political, and artistic) expression is. So "treat it like ad restrictions" smuggles in a category error: you can heavily regulate selling sneakers in a way you cannot regulate someone speaking about their beliefs.
- **History says this is the repressive move, not the liberating one.** Banning public religious expression is what the USSR, Maoist China, and Albania did — and it produced persecution, not peace. A rule indistinguishable in form from those regimes' should set off alarms.
- **It's unenforceable and infinitely contestable.** Is a church visible from the street "promotion"? A cross necklace? A charity run by a faith? "Merry Christmas" on TV? A philosophy professor discussing Aquinas? Drawing that line hands enormous, abusable discretion to whoever enforces it ([#05](../05-governance-democracy/)).

### The improved version (keeps your real aim, drops the rights violation)

Your underlying goal is sound: **stop religions from imposing themselves through public/state power, and stop religiously-justified harm.** You can get that *without* banning private/public expression by aiming at *power and coercion* instead of *speech*:

- **State neutrality, not silence.** The *state* and *public institutions* promote, fund, and favor no religion (no state church, no doctrine in public-school science class, no religious test for office, no public money for proselytizing). Individuals and communities remain free to express and even promote belief in public — that's their right.
- **No coercion, ever.** Ban forced conversion, religious coercion of children, and penalties for apostasy/unbelief. This is the real harm; target it precisely.
- **No doctrine taught as fact — especially to children.** Religion is not science and must **never** be taught *as established truth*, or in place of evidence, in any school — no catechism-as-fact, no creationism in the science room. Children have a right to an evidence-based education and to not be indoctrinated. *Teaching **about** religion* is the opposite thing and stays: comparative religion, its history and influence, studied descriptively and critically, is legitimate evidence-based knowledge (it is a fact that billions hold these beliefs and that this history happened), and it is how anyone comes to understand world events, art, and conflict ([#03](../03-education/), [#09](../09-conflict-peace/)). **Ban teaching-as-truth; keep teaching-about.** The principle "only present as knowledge what is evidence-based" forbids the first and *permits* the second.
- **Guard the loophole — "comparative" must actually be comparative.** The teaching-about carve-out gets abused: a class billed as neutral "religious education" that is, in practice, confessional single-faith instruction. This is not hypothetical. In **Germany**, public-school religious education is constitutionally *confessional* — taught "in conformity with the principles of the religious society concerned" (Grundgesetz Art. 7(3)), i.e. church-directed Catholic or Protestant doctrine. In **England**, the law requires the agreed syllabus to "reflect the fact that the religious traditions in Great Britain are **in the main Christian**" (Education Reform Act 1988, s.8(3)) — nominally multi-faith, in practice Christianity-weighted. So the rule needs teeth: **in public schools and other state institutions — and to any captive audience (pupils, prisoners, conscripts) — no teaching of a religion's doctrine *as the faith* (confessional instruction).** Any "teaching about religion" there must be genuinely balanced across traditions *and* non-belief, not one faith wearing a comparative label. (Consenting adults choosing to study a doctrine on their own time is a separate thing — that's voluntary, not the state instructing the captive.) **Teach *about*; never teach *into*.**
- **Narrow, defensible limits on *promotion*** — the parts of your instinct worth keeping: no state-sponsored religious promotion; no religious coercion or proselytizing imposed on a captive audience (public-school pupils, prisoners, soldiers); truth-in-claims and child-protection limits on aggressive proselytizing — the same narrow limits we'd put on *any* powerful persuader, secular or religious.
- **Symmetry clause.** The same protections apply to belief *and* non-belief: atheists, agnostics, and minority faiths get identical protection from coercion. This is what keeps the rule a *freedom* rule and not an *anti-religion* rule.

In short: **separation of church and state — yes, strongly. State neutrality — yes. A ban on people expressing or promoting belief in public — no; that's the move repressive regimes make, and it would violate the same Article 18 the project should be defending.**

## Metrics we'd watch

- Pew Government Restrictions Index and Social Hostilities Index (and their trend).
- Number of countries criminalizing apostasy/blasphemy; number with an official/established religion that confers legal privilege.
- Religiously-motivated violence and persecution (incidents, deaths, displaced).
- Religious freedom *and* freedom-from-religion measures — both directions.
- Degree of separation of religious authority from law and public schooling.

## Open questions

- Where exactly is the line between protected expression and harmful coercion? (The hard cases: proselytizing to children, religious exemptions that override others' rights, blasphemy vs. genuine incitement.)
- **Resolved (teaching scope).** An earlier draft flagged a disagreement over whether to ban *all* teaching of religion. Converged position: **teaching *about* religion (genuine, balanced comparative study) is allowed; teaching a religion's *doctrine as the faith* (confessional instruction) is banned in public schools and state institutions** — with an explicit guardrail against confessional instruction disguised as "comparative religion" (see the Germany/England examples above). The remaining live sub-question: **who certifies that a "comparative" syllabus is actually balanced** — without that certifier becoming its own capture surface ([#05](../05-governance-democracy/))?
- The **planned advertising rule referenced here does not yet exist** in this repo — it needs its own proposal under [`proposals/`](../../proposals/). Until it's written, "treat religion like advertising" has no defined meaning to inherit. Defining that rule (and deciding whether speech that is a *right* can ever be treated like commercial speech) is a prerequisite.
- How do deeply religious societies move toward state neutrality without it being experienced as an attack on identity (which fuels backlash)?
- Can the harm standard be defined tightly enough that it protects people without becoming a tool to suppress disfavored faiths?
