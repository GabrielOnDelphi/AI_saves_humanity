# 08 · Energy

**Status:** Draft · **Last updated:** 2026-06-16

## Scope

Getting clean, abundant, reliable, and affordable energy to everyone. Energy is a **master enabler**: it underlies health (#02), water (#07), education (#03), and economic opportunity (#01) — and the way we currently produce it is the main driver of climate change (#04). In scope: energy access/poverty, the clean-energy transition, grids and storage, and the politics of both.

## Key facts

- Hundreds of millions of people still have **no access to electricity**, and billions rely on polluting fuels (wood, charcoal, dung) for cooking — a major cause of indoor-air-pollution death (link to #02).
- Fossil fuels still supply the **large majority** of the world's primary energy despite rapid clean-energy growth — the transition has started but is far from finished.
- The **cost of solar, wind, and batteries has fallen dramatically** over the last 15 years, making clean power the cheapest new electricity in much of the world — the economics have flipped.
- **Energy demand is rising**, driven by development, electrification, cooling in a hotter world, and new loads like data centers/AI (link to #06).
- **More energy is generally good** for human welfare — the goal is not less energy but *clean* energy, abundantly.

## Root causes

- **A century of fossil infrastructure and incumbency.** Pipelines, plants, jobs, and political power are built around fossil fuels (link to #01, #05).
- **Externalized climate cost.** Fossil energy looks cheap because its damage isn't priced (link to #04).
- **Grid and storage bottlenecks.** Clean generation is cheap; integrating variable supply needs transmission, storage, and flexible demand we haven't built fast enough.
- **Access gaps.** The poorest lack capital and infrastructure for either fossil *or* clean energy.
- **Permitting and minerals.** Building clean energy fast runs into slow permitting and concentrated supply chains for critical minerals.

## Connections

- **↔ #04 Climate:** decarbonizing energy is most of the climate solution.
- **→ #02 Health:** clean cooking and power cut air-pollution deaths.
- **→ #07 Water / #03 Education / #01 Economy:** energy access enables pumping, lighting, study, and industry.
- **↔ #06 Existential risk:** soaring AI/data-center demand makes the clean-supply race harder.

## Candidate solutions (with trade-offs)

1. **Mass deployment of cheap clean generation** (solar, wind) + storage. *For:* now the cheapest option; fast to build. *Objection:* variability, grid, and minerals constraints; siting opposition.
2. **Modernize grids & build storage/transmission.** *For:* unlocks the clean generation we can already afford. *Objection:* slow permitting; high upfront capital; cross-jurisdiction coordination.
3. **Firm clean power** — nuclear (incl. advanced/SMRs), geothermal, hydro, long-duration storage. *For:* fills gaps when sun/wind don't deliver. *Objection:* cost and build-time (nuclear); siting and public acceptance.
4. **Universal clean-energy access** — distributed solar/microgrids, clean cooking. *For:* reaches the unserved poor and cuts indoor-air deaths. *Objection:* financing and maintenance in low-income regions.
5. **Price carbon / remove fossil subsidies** (see #04). *For:* levels the field for clean energy. *Objection:* political backlash if it raises near-term costs.

## Metrics we'd watch

- Share of population with electricity and clean cooking access.
- Clean energy as share of electricity and of total final energy.
- Cost per MWh of clean vs. fossil generation; battery cost/kWh.
- Grid storage and transmission capacity added per year.

## Open questions

- How fast can grids and storage scale to match cheap clean generation?
- What role for nuclear and other firm clean power in a mostly-renewable system?
- Can the developing world leapfrog straight to clean energy, or will it lock in fossils first?
