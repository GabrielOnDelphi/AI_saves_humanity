# 02 · Health & Disease

**Status:** Draft · **Last updated:** 2026-06-16

## Scope

Whether people can stay alive and well: access to care, the cost of getting sick, pandemics, the diseases of aging, and preventable death. In scope: health-system access and financing, infectious-disease control, non-communicable disease, aging, and biomedical progress. Closely tied to water/food (#07), mental health (#11), and inequality (#01).

## Key facts

- **~4.5 billion people** — more than half the world — are not fully covered by essential health services. *(WHO, 2024)*
- **~2 billion people** face financial hardship from health spending; over two decades, **~1.3 billion** were pushed into poverty by medical costs. *(WHO, 2024)*
- A child born in the highest-life-expectancy countries can expect to live **30+ years longer** than one born in the lowest — mostly from preventable causes.
- Antimicrobial resistance is rising; routine infections are becoming harder to treat, threatening the foundation of modern surgery and care.
- Aging populations mean chronic disease (heart disease, cancer, dementia) now dominates the global disease burden in most regions.

## Root causes

- **Money decides who lives.** Care tracks income — within and between countries. Out-of-pocket payment turns illness into bankruptcy (link to #01).
- **Workforce and infrastructure gaps.** Too few clinicians, clinics, and supply chains where need is highest; talent migrates to richer systems.
- **Misaligned incentives.** Health systems are paid to treat sickness, not to prevent it; R&D chases profitable drugs over high-burden, low-margin diseases.
- **Weak prevention.** Clean water (#07), nutrition, air quality (#04), and vaccination prevent more death than any cure, yet are chronically underfunded.
- **Pandemic underpreparedness.** Surveillance, vaccine manufacturing, and global coordination remain too slow for the next outbreak.

## Connections

- **← #01 Inequality:** ability to pay determines access.
- **← #07 Water/Food & #04 Climate:** dirty water, hunger, heat, and pollution are upstream causes of disease.

## Candidate solutions (with trade-offs)

1. **Universal health coverage (public financing / single-payer or hybrid).** *For:* removes the bankruptcy trap; strong outcome and equity evidence. *Objection:* cost, wait times, political resistance; quality depends on funding and governance.
2. **Shift money to prevention & primary care.** *For:* highest return per dollar; reduces downstream costs. *Objection:* benefits are diffuse and delayed, so they lose to acute-care politics.
3. **Pandemic preparedness as a permanent system** — global surveillance, distributed vaccine manufacturing, pre-negotiated sharing. *For:* one prevented pandemic pays for decades of cost. *Objection:* funded reactively, then forgotten.
4. **Fix R&D incentives** — advance market commitments, prizes, public/open drug development for neglected diseases and antibiotics. *For:* aims innovation at burden, not just margin. *Objection:* needs sustained public money and global buy-in.
5. **Health workforce investment & task-shifting** (community health workers, telemedicine). *For:* scalable where clinicians are scarce. *Objection:* quality assurance; not a substitute for specialists.

## Metrics we'd watch

- Share of population with essential-service coverage; out-of-pocket spending as % of health costs.
- Number pushed into poverty by medical bills.
- Healthy life expectancy (not just lifespan), and the gap between richest and poorest groups.
- Pandemic-response time: pathogen detection → vaccine availability.

## Open questions

- What mix of public/private financing delivers UHC without runaway cost or rationing?
- How do we fund prevention when its payoff is invisible and slow?
- Can biomedical breakthroughs (mRNA platforms, AI drug discovery) be steered toward global burden rather than rich-market diseases?
