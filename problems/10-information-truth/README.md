# 10 · Information & Truth

**Status:** Draft · **Last updated:** 2026-06-16

## Scope

Whether a society can know what is real and reason together about it. In scope: misinformation and disinformation, the attention economy, algorithmic amplification, media concentration, AI-generated content, and the erosion of shared facts. This is the **immune system of every other problem**: if a population can't tell true from false, it can't choose good leaders (#05), accept hard climate trade-offs (#04), or respond to a pandemic (#02, #06).

## Key facts

- Social platforms optimize for **engagement, and outrage/novelty out-engage accuracy** — so the incentive structure itself favors falsehood and division.
- Studies have found **false news spreads faster and wider** than true news on social networks — a structural, not accidental, property.
- **Generative AI** now makes convincing fake text, images, audio, and video cheap and scalable (link to #06), collapsing the cost of manufacturing "evidence."
- **Media ownership is concentrated** (link to #01): a few owners and platforms shape what billions see.
- Trust in news and institutions has **declined** in many countries, leaving people with no shared baseline of facts (link to #11, #05).

## Root causes

- **The attention/advertising business model.** Revenue depends on engagement, and the most engaging content is often the most inflammatory or false — incentives are misaligned with truth.
- **Algorithmic amplification.** Ranking systems boost whatever maximizes engagement, with no built-in weight on accuracy.
- **Cheap fabrication.** AI removes the last friction from producing fake content at scale.
- **Cognitive biases.** Confirmation bias and tribal identity make people *want* to believe congenial falsehoods.
- **Weak information literacy** (link to #03): many people were never taught how to evaluate sources or statistics.
- **Deliberate manipulation.** States and interests run disinformation campaigns because they work.

## Connections

- **→ #05 Governance:** a misinformed electorate can't hold power accountable, and can be steered.
- **← #03 Education:** critical thinking is the long-term defense.
- **↔ #06 Existential risk:** AI supercharges disinformation; disinformation cripples crisis response.
- **← #01 Inequality:** concentrated media ownership concentrates narrative power.

## Candidate solutions (with trade-offs)

1. **Change platform incentives** — rank for accuracy/quality, add friction, transparency on algorithms, and on advertising. *For:* attacks the root incentive. *Objection:* who defines "quality"? Risk of bias and censorship; platform resistance.
2. **Media & information literacy at scale** (link to #03). *For:* durable, empowers individuals, hard to abuse. *Objection:* slow; doesn't stop today's flood.
3. **Provenance & authenticity standards** — cryptographic content provenance, AI-content labeling. *For:* helps distinguish real from synthetic. *Objection:* gameable; adoption must be near-universal to matter.
4. **Independent, well-funded public-interest journalism.** *For:* rebuilds a shared factual baseline. *Objection:* funding model is broken; trust must be earned, not assumed.
5. **Transparency, not censorship** — regulate disclosure and manipulation rather than banning speech. *For:* preserves free expression. *Objection:* hard line to walk; defining "manipulation" can be abused.

## Metrics we'd watch

- Prevalence and reach of demonstrably false claims on major platforms.
- Trust in news; share of population with a basic media-literacy competency.
- Concentration of media/platform ownership.
- Adoption of content-provenance standards.

## Open questions

- Can platform incentives be fixed *without* handing someone dangerous power to define truth?
- How do open societies counter disinformation without becoming censorial?
- In a world of cheap AI fakes, what becomes the new basis for trusting evidence?
